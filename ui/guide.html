The solver takes the nine hardpoints of a double-wishbone corner, plus an optional pushrod, rocker and damper. It shows how the wheel moves through bump, roll and steering, what the spring and damper see, and how hard each tube is loaded. Every number updates as you type.

Five-minute start

  1. Open the app and leave the design menu on Example front. It is an invented demo car that passes every target.
  2. Read the summary strip: targets met, ride frequency, motion ratio and the lowest strength FOS. Anything failing is listed red in the Targets tab, and clicking a row explains the number.
  3. Drag the Travel and Rack sliders (or type a value, or press ▶ Play). The 3D corner moves, the readout shows camber, toe and roll-center height at that position, and the gold marker moves along every chart.
  4. Click Pin as baseline (under the sliders), then change one coordinate. Every target gains a Baseline and Change column, and the charts show the old curve dashed. That is how you learn what each hardpoint does.
  5. Save your work with Save ▾ → Save JSON, which writes a .json file to your computer (in Chrome or Edge you choose where, and later saves go back to the same file). Keep it in your team's private folder and load it again with Open ▾. The app also remembers your last edits in that browser.

Viewing controls: drag to orbit, right-drag or shift-drag to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom. The Front, Side and Top buttons give standard views, and Display ▾ turns on the roll-center lines, the other side of the car and joint names.

Keeping designs private

Your hardpoints never leave your computer. This site is public, but Open ▾ reads a file inside your browser and sends nothing anywhere. The only designs built into the site are the invented examples.
  • Keep team designs in a folder only your team can see, such as a shared drive or the private/ folder of your copy of the code (Git ignores it).
  • The app remembers your last unsaved edits on that computer only. Choosing a design from the menu replaces them.
  • Your own target ranges can travel with your designs. Put them in each design's targets, or in one shared team_targets.json that designs point to with "targets_file". In the app, open that targets file with Open ▾ to apply it to the design on screen.
  • Never commit design files to the public repository. Before each commit, git status should list no JSON outside designs/.

Front, rear and the whole car

The switch at the top right picks what you are working on. Front and Rear each edit one corner. Car (the default) puts both together:

  • Vehicle settings are shared. Mass, weight split, CG height, wheelbase and brake balance are entered once and used by both corners.
  • Anti-roll bars are entered as a rate at the wheels (Nm per degree of body roll; 0 = no bar). A bar adds roll stiffness without changing the ride frequency. The split of roll stiffness between front and rear sets how the lateral load transfer divides between the axles (LLTD), which is one of the main handling-balance knobs. More front LLTD than the front weight share pushes the car toward understeer.
  • Car targets: roll gradient (deg of body roll per g), front LLTD minus front weight share, and the rear/front ride-frequency ratio. The page also says what bar rates would hit the middle of your targets.
  • Export ▾ gives a printable design report and an Excel workbook of the whole car (or of one corner in the Front and Rear views), for design reviews and sharing outside the app.
  • Export ▾ → 3D for CAD… writes the hardpoints and links as an IGES or STEP file (a named 3D sketch in SolidWorks, Fusion or Onshape), a CSV point table, or STL tubes. Pick the axes that match your CAD model. The links at full bump, droop and steer come as extra layers, so the chassis designer can check frame tubes against the whole space the links sweep through.
  • Click a corner in the 3D car to edit it. In the Car view, Save JSON writes one car file with both corners; single-corner files still open into the front or rear slot.

Do you need a bar? Not always: the example car's springs alone already give a stiff roll gradient, but its LLTD sits below the front weight share, so it runs a front bar purely for balance. Pushrod or pullrod is a packaging choice: a pushrod (rod in compression, rocker and damper up high) is easier to package and service; a pullrod (rod in tension, so it can be thinner with no buckling risk; rocker and damper low) lowers that mass and frees space up top, but is harder to reach. The solver handles both: put the rod on the upper wishbone for a pullrod.

Learning the terms, and finding what to move

New to suspension? These tools explain each number on your own design, not on a textbook car.

  • Learn (top right): a searchable glossary of every term the app uses, such as scrub radius, roll center, anti-dive and motion ratio. Each card gives the sign convention, what moves it and its value on the design on screen.
  • Show me: draws the idea on the 3D corner. Examples are the steering axis meeting the ground, the wishbones extended to the instant center, and the wheel sweeping through bump while camber or toe changes. Names on the viewer labels every hardpoint.
  • Fix a failing target: in the Targets tab, click the row, then Fix: what moves this? The app tries every single-point move and the paired moves (both pivots of an arm, or the whole tie rod). It lists the smallest moves that bring the number into range, with the millimeters needed. Each move is re-solved and checked against every other target, and only targets it would newly break are listed as side effects. On a target that already passes, the same button shows the biggest levers on it.
  • Click a joint in the 3D view, or a hardpoint name in the inputs. A panel shows what moving that point 5 mm up, outboard or forward does to each metric. Blue cells go up, gold cells go down, darker means bigger, and a red outline marks a target that would start failing.
  • Undo / Redo (top right, or Cmd/Ctrl+Z and Shift+Cmd/Ctrl+Z when you are not typing in a box) step back through every change: typed values, target ranges, car settings, applied moves and opened designs.
  • Preview, Apply, Undo: Preview shows the moved corner as an orange ghost with a summary of the change. Apply makes the move and pins the old design as the baseline, so every change column shows what you gained. Undo steps back.
  • Ball joints, contact patch and rocker pivot are locked by default, because the upright, wheel and chassis set them. Untick the box to include them.

Challenges

Click Start here (top right), then Try a challenge, for two short exercises on the case-study car: Lift the roll center (move the four inboard pivots) and Kill the bump steer (move the tie-rod ends). The inputs column becomes a challenge panel and the results area shows only your goals as curves (Show full results brings the tabs back). The panel has the brief, live goal meters, only the points you may move, and three hints, all computed by the solver from your current geometry. Hint 1 explains the idea and draws it. Hint 2 is a live table of simple moves (for example "lower arm up 5 mm"), each shown in the direction that helps the goal furthest from target, best first, with how far you would have to move it. "Not alone" means that move helps but cannot finish the job by itself. Green cells get closer to target, red further away. Hint 3 tries every single move and pair of moves at 1 to 4 steps each way and suggests the best next step, which you can preview. It is a plain search with the solver, not AI. "What moves this?" is turned off during a challenge, and hint 3 unlocks the influence panel. Stars: one for meeting the goals, one for the bonus, one for moving no more than par. Your own design comes back when you exit.

Comparing designs

The Compare tab puts up to four saved designs next to the live one. Save the design on screen, add an example, or open design files. You get every target with the change from a reference you pick, the hardpoints that differ, overlaid curves, and colored ghosts in the 3D view. Copy table (CSV) pastes into a spreadsheet. Saved designs stay in your browser only.

Treat the suggestions as a starting point. They change one thing at a time and know nothing about frame tubes or bolt access. Check every move in CAD before you trust it.

Each stage changes things the earlier stages fixed, so work in this order and recheck everything after each one.

Case study: one front corner from first draft to all targets met

This is the real design history of the app's example car. Each step is in the app's design menu (Case study 1 to Case study 4), so you can load it and follow along. We used the optimizer to save time, but each pass moved only one group of points for one purpose. That is the same discipline as changing one coordinate by hand.

Goals for this pass: roll-center height, camber gain, anti-dive, and roll-center movement. We moved only the eight inboard pivot Y and Z coordinates:

The short-upper, long-lower arm pair is what stopped the roll center wandering: −387 → −0.2 mm/deg. The front-view swing arm grew from 2.0 m to 3.3 m, so camber gain fell to 0.45 deg/in, and the outer tire now leans with the body 0.81° per degree of roll.

Lesson: pivot heights move the roll center up and down. Arm lengths decide how far it wanders. Fix both before touching the tie rod, because moving the arms changes bump steer (here 1.59 → 0.09 deg/in without touching the tie rod).

Step 3 · Tie rod 19 of 20 pass

Goals for this pass: zero bump-steer slope, zero bump-steer curve, Ackermann near 18 %. We moved only the tie-rod ends:

Bump steer went from 0.094 deg/in to 0.000, with under 0.001° of toe change over ±30 mm. Ackermann fell from 33 % to 18 %, and the outer wheel's turning radius at full rack is 3.49 m.

Lesson: a straight toe-vs-travel line means the tie rod is at the wrong height. A U-shaped line means it is the wrong length or angle. The toe chart (Motion → Heave) shows which one you have.

Step 4 · Pushrod, rocker and damper 20 of 20 pass

The damper is a common 200 × 57 mm size (eye-to-eye × stroke). The rocker pivot and pushrod mount stayed where the chassis and arm put them. Only the two rocker arms and the damper's chassis mount moved, each by under 5 mm. The goals and results:

Lesson: damper stroke limits wheel travel. With 57 mm of stroke and a 0.80 motion ratio, ±30 mm of wheel travel uses 48 mm, so the damper must sit near mid-stroke at ride height or it will top out or bottom out.

Step 5 · Loads and the whole car

Strength. The Loads tab checks bump, cornering (outer and inner wheel), braking and combined braking and cornering. With 5/8 × 0.035 in 4130 tubes, the weakest member is the lower wishbone leg to the pivot at X = −230 in braking plus cornering: 2855 N compression against a 10 600 N buckling load, so FOS 3.7 against the required 2.0. The pushrod carries 3.0 kN in a 3 g bump; pick rod ends rated well above the "worst |force|" column.

Impacts and the pushrod mount. Two more cases, a 4 g pothole and a 2 g kerb strike, show how much harder an impact is on the parts than normal driving. Below the table, Pushrod mount bending checks the wishbone leg the pushrod sits on: the pushrod lands 17 mm off that leg's centerline, so the leg bends like a beam. In the pothole case the bare tube would reach about 780 MPa, FOS 0.56. The fix is a gusset or bracket that carries the pushrod into the ball-joint node, not a bigger tube. Rod ends and bolts then compares each member's worst force with your rod end's catalog rating and the bolt's shear capacity.

Compliance. Last on the Loads tab: every link stretches a little under load (rod ends, tube and chassis tab in series, about 6.5–7 kN/mm each here). In 1.6 g braking the front wheel toes out 0.13°, inside the 0.15° limit; a softer chassis tab would push it over. The rod-end and tab stiffnesses are estimates, so measure yours if the result is close to the limit.

Balance. With the matching example rear (3.6 Hz, 75 N/mm springs), the whole-car check (python3 susp_solver.py car designs/example_front.json designs/example_rear.json) gives 0.72 deg/g of roll from the springs alone. But only 44 % of the lateral load transfer is at the front, against 47 % of the weight on the front. That leans the car toward oversteer. The tool's fix: a front anti-roll bar of about 190 Nm/deg, or softer rear springs.

Lesson: each corner can pass every target and the car can still be unbalanced. Check the front and rear together before buying springs.

Common first-year mistakes

Axes from CAD

The solver wants X forward, Y outboard on the right wheel, Z up, ground at Z = 0. If the input checks complain about ball joints or the contact patch, your CAD axes are mapped wrongly. The command-line import --map option fixes that.

"Front" and "rear" pivots

Following KineSolver, the pivot labeled "front" is the one with the smaller X. With X forward, that is physically the rear pivot. The app labels each one "smaller-X pivot" to avoid confusion.

Fixing bump steer too early

Every change to the wishbones changes bump steer. Tune the pivots first, then the tie rod, then check again.

Checking clearance only at ride height

Links usually collide at full droop with full lock. The Packaging tab checks every combination of full droop, ride height and full bump with full left lock, center and full right lock.

Picking the damper last

Damper stroke and length decide how much wheel travel you can have. Choose the damper before you lay out the rocker.

Trusting one corner

Use the whole-car check before choosing springs and anti-roll bars.

How the numbers are checked

Every change to the solver is tested automatically on GitHub before it reaches this site:

  • Against KineSolver. On three real corners (two front, one rear), camber, caster, KPI, toe, roll center and anti-dive / squat / lift at full bump and droop all agree within about 0.06 mm and 0.04°, the precision KineSolver's charts show.
  • Against hand calculations. 22 cases with exact answers, such as:
    • equal parallel arms give zero camber gain and bump steer;
    • a roll center and swing arm built with a ruler;
    • caster, KPI, scrub and trail from the ball-joint coordinates;
    • a damper's motion ratio equals its lever ratio times the cosine of its angle;
    • the member forces hold the upright in exact equilibrium;
    • Euler buckling loads;
    • the car's load transfer adding up to m g h.
  • Browser against Python. The two versions of the solver agree to about 1e-11, so this page and the command-line tool give the same answers.
  • The web app itself is clicked through in a real browser: the challenges are solved by following the hints, the exports open, and the phone layout fits.

Not yet checked against an outside source: loads, ride and the whole-car balance (they are checked by physics identities only). If you have results from another tool or from your car on a rig, add them to the validation/ folder (instructions inside). They are then checked on every change.

What the solver does not do

  • The kinematic curves use rigid links. The Loads tab adds a separate compliance check (rod ends, tubes and tabs stretching); arm bending, bushings, upright and wheel flex, the tire and chassis twist are not modeled.
  • Roll is modeled as equal and opposite wheel travel, which gives the kinematic roll center.
  • Loads are quasi-static at ride height. Tabs, welds, inserts and fatigue are not checked.
  • The default ranges are starting points, not rules. Check wheelbase, travel and turning limits against the current FSAE rulebook.
  • Always confirm the final design in CAD.
